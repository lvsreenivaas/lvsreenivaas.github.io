Citi · UC Berkeley industry project · Supervisor: Peter Decrem

Hedging Italian sovereign risk with Bund futures, across regimes

Oct – Dec 2026

The question. Italian government bonds (BTPs) are routinely hedged with German Bund futures on a covariance ratio estimated over a long window. That ratio is a minimum-variance solution to a problem that assumes the Italy–Germany relationship is stable. It is not. The whole value of the hedge is concentrated in the days when the spread jumps — and those are precisely the days the static estimate is wrong about.

What I did. I formulated the hedge as a minimum-variance problem and proved its optimality conditions, then derived the jump-adjusted no-arbitrage drift for a regime-switching spread process. I modelled regime-specific volatility, correlation, expected holding time and transition jump sizes, and let the hedge ratio update on the inferred regime rather than on a single unconditional covariance.

The result that mattered was a spanning argument

In a five-regime model there are 20 possible transition shocks. A hedger holding seven futures contracts has seven instruments. A spanning argument shows those seven cannot cover all 20 — hedging arbitrary jumps requires at least 19 instruments. This isn't a calibration problem that more data fixes; it's a rank problem. It explains the 19% of initial risk that stayed BTP-specific no matter what we did with futures.

So we went nonlinear. If the futures can't span the jump space, the remaining lever is convexity. I optimised a three-option Bund structure against the nonlinear jump exposure, which cut residual jump variance by a further 13.6% across the 2018, 2020 and 2022 crisis episodes.

What I'd flag. Going from 7% to 9% variance reduction is not a headline, and I'd rather present it as one honest number than dress it up. The genuinely transferable output is the spanning result: it tells a desk how many instruments a regime-aware hedge needs before anyone spends time estimating ratios.

UC Berkeley MFE · with Aaryen Mehta and Tejas Girhe

Firms that look alike: graph neural networks and the cross-section of returns

Aug – Oct 2026

The question. There's a well-established literature showing return predictability flows along economic links — customers and suppliers, shared analysts, technological relatedness. Graph neural networks are the natural way to exploit that. But observed links aren't available for every firm in every period. So: can you build the graph out of firm characteristics instead, and does that graph add anything over a plain neural network that already sees the same characteristics?

Setup. U.S. stocks, 2010–2024, roughly 150,000 out-of-sample firm-months. 64 firm characteristics. TransformerConv models trained across six message-passing depths, against a characteristics-only neural network baseline. We tested both a simple nearest-neighbour graph and a learned graph.

The answer is a reframe, not a yes or a no

Message passing between characteristic-similar firms was not consistently superior to the plain network. The learned graph helped in one specification and not across others. The reason shows up in the raw data: the comovement between characteristic neighbours is largely accounted for by the characteristics that defined them, so there is little residual information left for the graph to transmit. Characteristic neighbours work better as a reference group than as a channel.

Ranking the same forecasts within 20 characteristic clusters took annualised volatility from 23% to 9% and the Sharpe ratio from 0.69 to 1.03 — no new information, just a better denominator. And a standalone peer-relative reversal signal (a firm's prior-month return against its 20 nearest neighbours) ran a gross Sharpe of 1.38, concentrated in the short leg and in small firms.

My contributions specifically. I fixed excess-return targeting and look-ahead bias in the Ridge/LASSO baselines; expanded the point-in-time graph from 940 to 3,138 counterparties and built an SEC filings pipeline that added 14,339 edge rows; ran the six-benchmark spanning tests (incremental signal at four of six depths); and diagnosed the deeper-model failure as oversmoothing rather than overfitting.

A reproduction note worth more than the result

The lead–lag computation is a float32 mean, and exact ties at the decile cut made results platform-dependent — the same code gave different deciles on different machines. Sorting each firm's neighbours by similarity before averaging reproduces the published series bit-exactly. Separately, 10 of the 64 characteristics turned out to be constant within every month, so only 54 actually vary cross-sectionally. Neither fact changes the headline; both change how much you should trust a single run.

The private-markets dataset that was public the whole time

2026

The observation. Every U.S. public pension fund posts its complete private-equity book every quarter. Fund name, vintage, commitment, paid-in, distributions, NAV, DPI, TVPI, net IRR — all of it, in board meeting materials, free to anyone. Across 28+ funds that's a $4T+ addressable universe of LP-reported performance. PitchBook and Preqin charge $20K–$250K a year for a worse version of the same information.

Why nobody had built it

Three barriers, none of them technical. The data is fragmented across 90+ sites, each with its own format and schedule — no API, no bulk export, no single source. It's PDF-native rather than data-native, living inside board packets as tables. And the toolset to extract it (Python, pdfplumber, regex, Tesseract) has been free for a decade. The scarce resource was never the engineering. It was recognising that LP-reported net IRR is categorically more accurate than anything a vendor aggregates from GP self-reports.

What unsupervised LLMs got wrong

We started where everyone starts: paste the PDF, ask the model, get the IRR. It does not work, and no amount of prompt engineering fixed it. Three distinct failure modes:

  • Tables are not text. pdfplumber reads left-to-right across the page, so a four-column PE schedule arrives as garbled interleaved rows. A language model cannot reconstruct the original grid from a character stream.
  • Format heterogeneity. Verus reports differ fundamentally from Callan, NEPC, Aksia and StepStone — different column order, header names and footnote placement.
  • Hallucination at scale. On long board packets the models invented IRR values, swapped DPI and TVPI, and pinned performance to the wrong vintage year. No warning, no error flag. Just wrong numbers that look right — the worst possible failure mode for a dataset you intend to run regressions on.

The fix: deterministic code where it matters, intelligence where it scales. Seven consultant-specific parsers (Verus, Callan, NEPC, Aksia, Meketa, StepStone, AON), bounding-box extraction rather than linear text, and a regex normalisation layer mapping raw text into a nine-field schema per fund record. Scanned packets with no text layer get rasterised and run through Tesseract OCR, then re-enter the same pipeline. Footnotes bleeding into the last fund row — corrupting NAV and DPI with disclaimer text — were killed by a two-pass scan that detects the footnote boundary via font-size drop and clips above it. And number chaos got its own layer: (14.2) became -14.2, currency and percent symbols stripped, and N/A, an em dash, an empty string and a null collapsed into one NULL. Whatever survived that and still broke got a hardcoded rule.

The output lands in Postgres behind a FastAPI backend, with a Retool UI as the human QA and validation layer. The schema is modelled on PCRI — the Private Capital Research Institute dataset that Harvard, Stanford and Yale researchers use as the academic gold standard — which is roughly 4,000 funds from 40 LP contributors, updated infrequently, academic access only. Same schema. More funds. Better sourcing. Fully automated.

Then we benchmarked it against what people pay for

The interesting half of the project wasn't the pipeline — it was the head-to-head against PitchBook and Preqin on coverage density, sparsity, accounting consistency and reporting fidelity.

Vendor records fail arithmetic that cannot fail

TVPI = DPI + RVPI. That's an accounting identity, not an empirical claim. Vendor records violate it — stale fields, asynchronous updates and reconciliation errors produce fund rows that look coherent and are not. We also found "Frankenstein" records: semantically identical funds that the vendor failed to reconcile, so a single row is stitched together from reporting cycles months apart. Aggregate correlation with primary data stays high, which is exactly what makes this dangerous — the distortion is concentrated at the fund level, in unrealised valuations, which is where capital allocation decisions actually happen.

Making the two sources comparable at all

Before you can compare, you have to fix the clock. PE funds report at different frequencies (quarterly, semiannual, effectively annual) with different lags, so two values observed on different dates may describe completely different points in a fund's life. A 1.2x TVPI at age 2 is not a 1.2x TVPI at age 6. Comparing unmatched timing mistakes a reporting artifact for performance. So every dated observation gets mapped onto a fund-age axis (report year minus vintage year), medians are taken per age cell, and only cells with at least five funds in each source enter the comparison — 651 buyout observations from 429 funds and 338 VC observations from 219 funds on our side, against 2,904 and 3,476 Preqin observations respectively.

Fund paths versus snapshots. The second test was fund-level rather than aggregate. Our data often carries repeated dated marks for the same fund; Preqin typically contributes one. So the fair comparison isn't curve-versus-curve, it's path-versus-point: fit a cubic spline to our observations only, evaluate it at Preqin's single snapshot date, and compare. Of 53 modelled fund paths (31 buyout, 22 VC with at least four marks), 16 cases had the Preqin date falling strictly inside our observation window — true interpolation, the cleanest possible evidence. Those residuals were small: mean absolute 0.040x, median 0.020x, interquartile range −0.023x to +0.002x. Extrapolation cases (37 of them) were reported separately, at 0.093x.

The limitation became the finding

Preqin's fund-level snapshots are not systematically wrong — on clean same-date comparisons they're close. What they are is sparse as a time series. One dated point per fund means you cannot build a Preqin fund-level trajectory at all. So the honest conclusion isn't "our data is more accurate"; it's that the two sources support different questions. Ours supports trajectories; Preqin supports checkpoints. Downstream analytics that need movement over time — spline interpolation, J-curve modelling — structurally require primary data to function.

Corsair Capital · UC Berkeley MFE industry project · with Aaryen Mehta

Software-cycle regimes and private-equity returns — anatomy of a result that wasn't there

Mar – Sep 2026

The question. Do software-market conditions at the time a PE fund invests predict the fund's eventual TVPI? It's a natural question with an obvious research design, and the obvious research design gives you a significant answer. It took most of the project to work out that the answer was an artifact.

Construction. A 1,493-fund panel built from 462 pension filings. Ten correlated software-market indicators reduced by PCA to five components explaining 92.1% of variance. A three-state Gaussian hidden Markov model selected by BIC, classifying each quarter from 2011 Q1 to 2026 Q2 as contraction, neutral or expansion. Then: regress winsorised TVPI on fund age, age², and regime dummies, and test whether the regime dummies add explanatory power.

Sixty-two quarters is not sixty-two observations

The HMM produces five contiguous episodes with a mean length of 12.4 quarters. Regime is therefore almost a relabelling of calendar time — the mean dominant-regime share inside a deal entry year is 98%. And because the single expansion episode is also the most recent one, "invested during an expansion" and "is a young fund" are structurally confounded.

You can see the confound directly in the exit rates. Deal exit rate falls monotonically from 33.0% for 2014 entries to 0.2% for 2024 entries — that's pure exposure time. Sliced by regime it reads 20.6% neutral, 4.2% contraction, 0.4% expansion. Same column, relabelled.

The contradiction that forced the issue

Two inference procedures on the same 15 vintage clusters disagreed. Vintage- clustered standard errors said significant (p = 0.011 / 0.023) and described themselves as the honest inference. Vintage-permutation on the identical grouping said null (p = 0.141). Both can't be right.

Clustered sandwich standard errors are asymptotic in the number of clusters, not the number of observations. With G = 15 they over-reject — this is the Bertrand–Duflo–Mullainathan result, and the Cameron–Gelbach–Miller rule of thumb wants G in the 30–50 range before you trust them. The permutation test makes no such asymptotic assumption. The permutation test wins.

So I built a ladder of nulls

The cleanest way to show what's happening is to hold the estimand fixed — ΔR² of the regime dummies over tvpi_w ~ age + age², observed value 0.00351 against a baseline R² of 0.1253 — and vary only what the null hypothesis is permitted to permute.

Monotone p is a diagnostic

When p climbs steadily as the null respects more of the data's real dependence structure, you are not looking at a weak effect. You are looking at an inference-level error: the earlier, lower p-values were borrowing significance from units that were never independent. The episode-level null is 1.7× wider than the naive one. And there are only 50 distinct randomisations available at that level, which puts a hard floor of p = 0.020 on anything this design can ever detect.

What the deal-level analysis added. A parallel 73,806-company Preqin deal panel gave p < 0.0001, which looks decisive and isn't: it's a Moulton problem stacked on the exposure-time artifact above. I'd also flag that the fund-level universe (LP disclosures) and the deal-level universe (the full Preqin tech universe) were never matched to each other — they answer different questions about different populations, and the write-up needs to say so rather than let the reader assume continuity.

What would actually fix the design

Lengthen the estimation window back to 1995, which takes the episode count from ~5 to ~15 and the floor p from 0.020 to roughly 0.007. Break the age–regime confound by restricting to funds aged 8+ or switching the outcome to DPI on realised capital. Match the Preqin deals to the LP fund universe so the two analyses describe one population. And refit the HMM on expanding windows with filtered rather than smoothed posteriors — the current fit uses the full window, which is an in-sample association and biases towards finding an effect, making the null result stronger rather than weaker.

The contribution, stated honestly. The deliverable is the negative result plus its anatomy: a regime variable estimated on a sixteen-year window has roughly five independent realisations, and conducting inference at the fund, vintage or deal level manufactures significance that the data does not contain. That is a reusable warning about an entire class of regime-conditioning studies, which I'd argue is worth more than a p-value of 0.045 would have been.

AllShifts · Data Engineering & Revenue Analyst

Forecasting and credit systems at a healthcare staffing marketplace

Oct 2023 – Feb 2026

Two and a half years of turning messy operational data into things the business actually ran on. Less glamorous than research, and the single best training I've had in the gap between a model that validates and a model that survives contact with users.

  • Stochastic pricing optimiser. Facility-by-nurse-type ARIMA demand scenarios combined with seasonal rate fill curves, maximising expected fills subject to contract and margin constraints. Forecasts validated against 2024–2025 actuals.
  • Credit risk model. Logistic regression on 6- and 12-month delinquency and billing-to-payment histories. Validated on 400 clients, deployed to 1,700+. Post-rollout, defaults fell 17% and payments arrived two weeks sooner.
  • Cash collection forecasting. LightGBM on billing lags, accounts-receivable aging cohorts and calendar effects, predicting daily collections one to three weeks out at 4.8% out-of-sample MAPE.
  • Revenue-risk monitor. A four-week rolling view using STL to strip seasonality and LightGBM to flag client-level declines in orders, billings and payments before they showed up in the headline number.

Poker equity calculator

Ongoing

A Streamlit Texas Hold'em equity calculator built on Monte Carlo simulation via treys. It models opponents as random hands, returns equity against that range, and converts it into a pot-odds / expected-value call-or-fold recommendation. Modelling opponents as uniformly random is the obvious limitation and the obvious next thing to fix — range construction is where the real work is.
