Working paper · SSRN · October 2026

Firms That Look Alike: Characteristic Graphs and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns

Aaryen Milan Mehta · Tejas Girhe · Venkata Sreenivaas Lingam
UC Berkeley, Haas School of Business

View on SSRN Download PDF Project write-up

Abstract

Graph neural networks (GNNs) forecast stock returns by passing information between economically or statistically linked firms. We ask whether links inferred from 64 firm characteristics can improve return forecasts relative to a neural network that uses the same characteristics without a graph. In U.S. stocks from 2010 to 2024, a simple neighbor graph is not consistently superior to a basic neural network, and a learned neighbor graph results in a positive annual-return difference for one specification, but not consistently across a range of specifications. This is mirrored in the raw comovement between characteristic neighbours, with the characteristics used to define them accounting for much of the information in the raw data, leaving less information to be leveraged by graph-based message passing. This may be because characteristic-based neighbours are more likely to serve as a reference group than as a source of extra information: in the reported specification, the annualised volatility of the plain network drops from 23% to 9%, while the Sharpe ratio increases from 0.69 to 1.03 when ranking the forecasts within 20 characteristic clusters. A strategy using the previous month's return of a firm compared with its 20 closest characteristic-based neighbours shows peer-relative return reversal with a gross Sharpe ratio of 1.38, and the signal is focused in the short leg and small firms.

Keywords: graph neural networks, firm similarity, return predictability, short-term reversal, look-ahead bias.  ·  JEL: C45, C55, G11, G12, G14.

What I contributed

  • Fixed excess-return targeting and look-ahead bias in the Ridge/LASSO baselines.
  • Expanded the point-in-time counterparty graph from 940 to 3,138 firms, and built an SEC filings pipeline contributing 14,339 edge rows.
  • Ran six-benchmark spanning tests across message-passing depths.
  • Diagnosed the deep-model failure as oversmoothing rather than overfitting — prediction dispersion collapsed from 0.035 at depth one to 0.0017 at depth ten while train and test return correlations stayed equal at 0.081.

For the longer methodological discussion, including the float32 tie-breaking issue that made decile cuts platform-dependent, see the project write-up.

In progress

Robustness work on the paper is ongoing — in particular, re-running the within-cluster depth-1 comparison across multiple random seeds, where the measured gain is considerably less stable than a single run suggests. I'd rather publish the corrected version than the flattering one.

Other active threads: extending the private-markets panel backwards to lengthen the regime estimation window (see the regime project for why that matters), and continued work on statistical arbitrage and volatility research.
